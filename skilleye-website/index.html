The insight

Ball trajectory can't tell you why a shot went wrong.

Consumer tools like SwingVision answer “did the shot land in.” But movement quality — what the body did — is the input a coach actually reasons about, and it isn't derivable from where the ball went.

What existing apps see

Ball tracking and scorekeeping. Outcome, not cause. A player learns that they missed, never why — no read on elbow position, hip timing, or follow-through.

What SkillEye sees

A normalized 2D skeleton per swing, modeled over time, compared against expert-clip templates — surfacing the specific joint and phase where the motion drifted, with a plain-language correction.

How it works

One skeleton representation, from raw swing to coaching cue.

A single pose sequence drives every downstream task — stroke type, skill level, and per-phase quality scoring — following two established lines of work: RTMPose for real-time pose, ST-GCN for skeleton-based modeling.

01

Swing video

A short clip of a single stroke, phone or fixed camera.

02

Pose extraction

RTMPose → primary-subject tracking → hip-anchored, torso-scaled COCO-17 keypoints.

03

ST-GCN model

Spatio-temporal graph conv over the skeleton graph — spatial edges follow the body, temporal edges follow each joint through time.

04

Quality score

Phase detection + per-joint deviation from expert templates, as z-scores flagged by phase.

05

Correction feedback

Rule-based cues, optionally rephrased by an LLM into one coaching paragraph — grounded only in flagged deviations.

Sensor fusion. A wrist-worn IMU adds motion the camera can't see — angular velocity through the swing — fused with the visual skeleton to sharpen phase timing and catch off-plane rotation.

The wearable

Two revisions of a wrist IMU, designed from scratch.

Custom KiCad boards pairing an inertial sensor with a microcontroller and LiPo power — shrinking from an ESP32 prototype to a compact XIAO-based v2.
